31. Part IV at a glance¶

31.1 What this part covers¶

Parts I to III worked with numbers on paper: soundings, spectra and weather maps. This part goes into the laboratory. In two sessions (Weeks 8 to 11) you test, with your own hands, the four physical ideas that the rest of the book depends on (EGM305 module team n.d.):

Density

how much mass is packed into a volume, and why warming makes air and water lighter (Chapter 32).

Heat and temperature

the difference between them, the three ways heat moves, and the latent heat hidden in melting and evaporation (Chapter 33).

Pressure

force spread over an area, why it falls with height, and why gases squeeze while water does not (Chapter 34).

Buoyancy

why things lighter than their surroundings rise, the principle behind every rising cloud and every stable or unstable atmosphere (Chapter 35).

Many of the experiments use water instead of air, because water’s motions can be seen with a few drops of dye. The physics is identical in both fluids, and every activity ends by carrying the result back into the atmosphere. The activities follow the inquiry-based approach of Karp-Boss et al. (2009); the atmospheric background follows Ahrens and Henson (2022) and Wallace and Hobbs (2006).

Chapters 32 to 35 build the physics; Chapters 36 and 37 take each activity step by step, answer every question in the handbook, and give worked numbers you can compare with your own; Chapter 38 gathers all the answers, the mistakes to avoid and a self-test. The constants, symbols and corrections to the handbook are in Appendix D. A six-page lab card made from the same material is designed to be carried to the bench.

31.2 How the sessions run¶

You work in a team. Each session has three experiments of 30 to 45 minutes; some are split into two or three short activities. Share the jobs (one person reads the thermometers, one writes, one handles the equipment) and swap round, so everyone tries everything. By the second session your team should run smoothly.

What to record for every experiment

  1. Aim, hypothesis or question: what you are testing, in one sentence.

  2. Materials and set-up: a labelled sketch.

  3. Method: the steps you actually followed.

  4. Predictions: written before you test, each with a reason.

  5. Observations, measurements and calculations: numbers with units, in a table; a graph where asked.

  6. Conclusion and explanation: was the prediction right, and which physics explains what you saw?

  7. Examples in meteorology or oceanography where the same process works.

Writing the prediction first is the heart of the method. Science advances by predicting, asking, measuring, generalising and testing by application; a prediction that turns out wrong is as useful as one that turns out right, provided you can explain why.

Safety and etiquette

Wear a buttoned lab coat and sensible shoes, tie back long hair, and stand while you work. Bags go under the benches; keep the aisles clear. Report every accident, however small. Keep paper, sleeves and hair away from the spirit lamps, matches and the burning teabag, and use the non-flammable mat. Phones, laptops and tablets stay off and in your bag: anyone seen using one is asked to leave and marked absent. There are no spare parts, so handle the vacuum flasks, thermometers and balls gently. Leave the equipment and bench as clean as you found them, or cleaner, and wash your hands with soap at the end.

Bring: a lab book (squared paper makes graphs easier), a calculator, pencil, ruler and eraser, a tea towel and a lab coat.

31.3 Conventions for this part¶

The conventions of the whole book are in § 1.3. In this part:

  • Temperatures are in degrees Celsius unless a formula needs kelvin: \(T_{\mathrm{K}} = T_{\ensuremath{^{\circ}\mathrm{C}}} + 273.15\).

  • Densities are in kg m\(^{-3}\); in the lab you will often measure g cm\(^{-3}\) (multiply by 1000).

  • Worked numbers use typical classroom values. Your own measurements will differ: compare the pattern, not the last digit.
