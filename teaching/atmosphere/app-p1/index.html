A.2 Derivations for the curious

A.2.1 Pressure falls exponentially with height

Start from hydrostatic balance, Eq. (3.2), written for a very thin layer, \(dp/dz=-\rho g\), and replace the density using the gas law, Eq. (3.1), \(\rho=p/(R_dT)\): \[\frac{dp}{dz}=-\frac{g}{R_dT}\,p\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\frac{dp}{p}=-\frac{dz}{H},\qquad H=\frac{R_dT}{g}.\] The rate of fall of \(p\) is proportional to \(p\) itself. If \(T\) (and so \(H\)) is taken as constant, integrating from sea level (\(z=0\), \(p=p_0\)) to height \(z\) gives \(\ln(p/p_0)=-z/H\), that is \[p=p_0\,e^{-z/H},\] which is Eq. (3.3). The pressure halves when \(e^{-z/H}=\tfrac12\), at \(z_{1/2}=H\ln2=0.693\,H\); the 5.6 km corresponds to \(H=8.08\) km. Writing the same law for two levels and dividing, \(p_1/p_2=e^{(z_2-z_1)/H}\), so \(\ln(p_1/p_2)=(z_2-z_1)/H\): rearranged, this is Eq. (8.1), which gives the scale height of a layer from two lines of a sounding.

Without calculus. Divide the atmosphere into thin slabs, each \(\Delta z\) thick. By Eqs. (3.2) and (3.1), crossing one slab lowers the pressure by \(\Delta p=\rho g\,\Delta z=p\,\Delta z/H\): the same fraction \(\Delta z/H\) of whatever pressure there is. Removing the same fraction again and again is exactly what an exponential does.

A.2.2 The dry adiabatic lapse rate and potential temperature

The first law of thermodynamics for one kilogram of air says that heat added goes into warming the air and into the work it does as it expands: \(dq=c_p\,dT-dp/\rho\). In an adiabatic process no heat is added, \(dq=0\), so \[c_p\,dT=\frac{dp}{\rho}.\] For air moving vertically through an atmosphere in hydrostatic balance, \(dp=-\rho g\,dz\), Eq. (3.2). Substituting, \[c_p\,dT=-g\,dz\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\frac{dT}{dz}=-\frac{g}{c_p}=-\frac{9.81}{1004}=-0.0098\ \mathrm{K\,m^{-1}},\] which is Eq. (8.2): dry air cools by 9.8 \(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) per km as it rises and warms by the same amount as it sinks. Alternatively, dividing \(c_p\,dT=dp/\rho\) by \(T\) and using \(1/\rho=R_dT/p\) gives \(c_p\,dT/T=R_d\,dp/p\); integrating from \((T,p)\) to \((\theta,1000\ \ensuremath{\mathrm{hPa}})\), \[c_p\ln\frac{\theta}{T}=R_d\ln\frac{1000}{p}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\theta=T\left(\frac{1000}{p}\right)^{R_d/c_p},\] which is Eq. (8.3), with \(R_d/c_p=287/1004=0.286\) (Wallace and Hobbs 2006, ch. 3).

A.4 About the figures and the data

Every figure in Part I was drawn for it in the document itself with TikZ and pgfplots, from the data and the equations in the text.

  • The halving-rule curves (Figures 3.2 and 4.1) are Eq. (3.4); the dashed comparison curve and the layered profile of Figure 5.1 are the U.S. Standard Atmosphere (NOAA et al. 1976).

  • The soundings (Figures 6.1, 7.1 and 8.1) plot the values of Tables 6.1 and 7.1, joined by straight lines.

  • The potential temperatures (Figure 8.2) are computed from the soundings with Eq. (8.3), and the oxygen partial pressures (Figure 4.2) with Eq. (4.1).

  • The hydrostatic slab (Figure 3.1) is a diagram of the forces in Eq. (3.2).

  • The tropopause band of Figure 7.2 and the ocean-current sketch of Figure 9.1 are schematic: they show the pattern, not measurements.

The soundings are single ascents on a single day; they illustrate the structure of the atmosphere and should not be read as climatological averages (§ 9.4).
