33. Heat and temperature¶

33.1 Temperature is not heat¶

Temperature says how warm or cold something is relative to a standard. Physically it measures the average kinetic energy of the molecules’ motion. On the Celsius scale water freezes at 0\(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\)and boils at 100\(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\)at sea-level pressure; the Kelvin scale starts at absolute zero, \(T_{\mathrm{K}} = T_{\ensuremath{^{\circ}\mathrm{C}}} + 273.15\) (the triple point of water is 273.16 K, 0.01\(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\)). Temperature is never measured directly: thermometers read its effects, the expansion of a liquid in a bulb thermometer or the change in electrical resistance of a thermistor.

Heat is energy in transit from a warmer body to a cooler one, measured in joules (1 calorie = 4.18 J; the food Calorie is 1000 calories). Heat always flows from higher to lower temperature, but that is not the same as from more to less energy: a cup of hot tea has the higher temperature, a cold lake far more internal energy, simply because it is so large. Energy is conserved: what one body loses, the other gains.

33.2 Heat capacity: why the ocean is the climate’s flywheel¶

The specific heat capacity \(c\) is the heat needed to warm one kilogram by one degree. The heat to change a mass \(m\) by \(\Delta T\) is

\[ \tag{33.1} Q = m\,c\,\Delta T. \]

where
\(Q\) heat J
\(c\) specific heat capacity water 4186, air 1006 J kg\(^{-1}\) $^{\circ}\mathrm{C}

$$ ^{-1}$ |

| \(\Delta T\) | temperature change | \(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\)(or K: the same size of step) |

Water’s \(c\) is about four times air’s. Per volume the difference is far larger, because water is about 800 times denser than air:

Heat to warm one cubic metre by 1°C

Water: \(998 \times 4186 = 4.18\times10^{6}\) J. Air: \(1.225 \times 1006 = 1.23\times10^{3}\) J. Ratio \(\approx 3400\).\ So it takes about 3400 times more heat to warm a cubic metre of water than a cubic metre of air by the same amount, and water releases that much more when it cools. (The handbook rounds this to “about 4000”.)

Water also stores far more heat than rock or soil. So land warms and cools quickly, the ocean slowly: coastal places have milder days, nights and seasons than inland places; the ocean is a buffer that slows any change in the Earth’s temperature; and by carrying warm water poleward (the Gulf Stream) it evens out the difference between equator and poles, which would otherwise be much larger.

33.3 Three ways heat moves¶

Radiation¶

Every object emits electromagnetic radiation and absorbs some of what falls on it. If it absorbs faster than it emits, it warms; if it emits faster, it cools; when the two balance it sits at its equilibrium temperature. A black body absorbs all radiation falling on it; it emits, per square metre and per second, $$

\tag{33.2} E = \sigma T^4, \qquad \sigma = 5.67\times10^{-8}\ \ensuremath{\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}}\,\mathrm{K}^{-4},

\[ so doubling the temperature (in kelvin) multiplies the emission by $2^4 = 16$. The wavelength of peak emission falls as temperature rises (Wien’s law): \]

\tag{33.3} \lambda_{\max} = \frac{2898\ \ensuremath{\mu\mathrm{m\,K}}}{T}.

$$

Three emitters

The Sun, about 6000 K: \(\lambda_{\max} = 2898/6000 = 0.48\ \ensuremath{\mu\mathrm{m}}\), visible light. A heat lamp’s filament, about 2800 K: \(1.0\ \ensuremath{\mu\mathrm{m}}\), near infrared, felt as warmth. The Earth’s surface, 288 K: \(10\ \ensuremath{\mu\mathrm{m}}\), thermal infrared. At 300 K a black body emits \(5.67\times10^{-8}\times300^4 = 459\) \(\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\).

This is the basis of the greenhouse effect: the atmosphere lets most of the short-wave sunlight through but absorbs much of the long-wave infrared the surface emits, and sends part of it back down. Part II works all of this in detail (Chapters 13, 14 and 17).

Conduction¶

Conduction passes heat between bodies in contact, molecule to molecule: fast-vibrating molecules of the warmer body jostle the slower ones of the cooler. The rate grows with the contact area, with the temperature difference across the material, and with the material’s thermal conductivity: metals conduct well, wood, cloth and especially still air poorly.

Convection¶

Convection carries heat by moving the fluid itself, and happens only in liquids and gases. Warmed fluid becomes less dense and buoyant (Chapter 35), rises, and is replaced by cooler fluid, setting up a circulation. It is the main way heat moves in the atmosphere and ocean: from thermals and thunderstorms to the three-cell circulation of the globe.

33.4 Latent heat¶

Adding heat does one of two things: it raises the temperature, or it changes the state (solid to liquid, liquid to gas) at constant temperature. The heat for a change of state is the latent heat: $$

\tag{33.4} Q = m\,L, \qquad L_f = 334\ \mathrm{J\,g^{-1}}\ \text{(melting)}, \quad L_v = 2260\ \mathrm{J\,g^{-1}}\ \text{(evaporating at 100}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\text{)}.

\[ Going back (condensing, freezing) releases the same heat to the surroundings. Melting 1 g of ice takes as much heat as warming 1 g of water by $334/4.186 = 80 \]

^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$.

Latent heat in the climate system

  • Water evaporating from the ocean carries latent heat into the atmosphere; it is released where the vapour condenses into cloud, warming the air. This powers thunderstorms and hurricanes, and is a main reason seas are rarely warmer than 28 to 30\(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\).

  • In polar seas, freezing in winter releases heat and melting in summer absorbs it, so surface temperatures there change little through the year. Ice keeps a drink cold until it has all melted.

  • Sweating cools us: evaporating water takes its latent heat from the skin. That is why you shiver leaving a pool on a hot day.

Does water have to boil to evaporate?

No. In a liquid, molecules move at a spread of speeds; the fastest can break free at any temperature, which is why puddles and towels dry. Because the fast ones leave, the average energy of those left behind falls: evaporation cools the liquid. Warming the liquid gives more molecules enough energy, so evaporation speeds up. Ice can even evaporate directly (sublimation).

33.5 Thermal expansion¶

Most substances expand when warmed and contract when cooled, because their molecules vibrate harder and sit further apart. It is why a heated gas fills more volume (and so is less dense), and in the ocean it is a major cause of sea-level rise: estimates put thermal expansion at roughly a quarter to a half of the observed rise, although decadal climate swings make its exact share hard to pin down.
