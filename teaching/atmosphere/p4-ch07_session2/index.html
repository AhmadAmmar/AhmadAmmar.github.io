37. Session 2: pressure and buoyancy¶

37.1 Activity 2.1: expanding balloons¶

Set-up and steps

Materials: three distillation flasks with bungs, a vacuum pump, balloons, water, a marshmallow, a cherry tomato, matches.

  1. Put a balloon filled with air into the first flask and one filled with water into the second (how will you do it?). Predict the effect of reduced pressure on each.

  2. Pump the air out of the first flask. What happens to the balloon? Remove the vacuum tube to let air back in. What happens now?

  3. Repeat with the water balloon. Does pressure affect the two balloons differently? Why?

  4. What will happen to the marshmallow and the cherry tomato when the flask is evacuated?

  5. Test your prediction.

  6. Release the valve and watch the marshmallow. Explain.

  7. What would happen to all four objects if you increased the pressure with a bicycle pump?

  8. What happens to the temperature inside the air-filled balloon as the pressure around it rises or falls?

  9. Use what you have learned to make a cloud in the flask. Which ingredients are needed?

  10. Take the balloon out of the flask so the next group can struggle to get it in.

Getting a balloon into a flask. Push the deflated balloon in, keep its neck over the mouth, blow it up and tie it off inside; for the water balloon, fill it inside the flask from a tap or a funnel. Both then sit at room pressure.

The physics. Boyle’s law (34.4): the air in the balloon keeps \(pV\) constant, so when the pressure around it falls it expands until its pressure again matches its surroundings (plus the small extra the stretched rubber adds). Water hardly compresses or expands at all.

How much does the air balloon grow?

A hand pump typically lowers the flask’s pressure to about half an atmosphere. At constant temperature \(V_2 = V_1\,p_1/p_2 = V_1 \times 1/0.5 = 2V_1\): the balloon tries to double its volume, a \(2^{1/3} = 1.26\) times larger diameter. The rubber’s tension holds it back somewhat, so it grows a little less.

Answers

1–3. The air balloon swells as the air is pumped out and shrinks back when air is let in. The water balloon does not change visibly: water is almost incompressible (§ 34.4).\ 4–6. The marshmallow is a foam of tiny air bubbles: it swells under vacuum. When air is let back in it shrinks, and usually ends up smaller and wrinkled, because the stretched bubble walls tore while expanded, so some bubbles cannot refill. The cherry tomato is mostly water: little change, perhaps a slight swelling of air pockets near the stalk.\ 7. With higher pressure everything with gas inside shrinks: the air balloon and the marshmallow are squeezed, while the water balloon and tomato barely change.\ 8. Expanding air does work and cools; compressed air warms (34.5). Pumped out quickly, the air in the flask cools noticeably for a moment before the glass warms it again.\ 9. A cloud needs three things: moisture (a little water in the flask to humidify the air), condensation nuclei (smoke from a blown-out match), and cooling (a sudden expansion: pump out quickly, or let a compressed flask release suddenly). The air cools below its dew point and a cloud appears; let the pressure back and the cloud evaporates.

In the atmosphere

A rising air parcel meets lower pressure, expands and cools, about 10\(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\)per kilometre while unsaturated. When it reaches its dew point, cloud forms on the countless dust, salt and smoke particles in the air. Your ears “pop” in a climbing aircraft for the same reason the balloon swells: the air inside them is at a higher pressure than the air outside.

37.2 Activity 2.2: the Archimedes ball¶

Set-up and steps

Materials: the Archimedes ball (a hollow plastic sphere with a stopper), a 60 mL syringe, tubing, scales, calliper, a container of water and one of an “unknown liquid”.

  1. Imagine the ball is a submarine. Calculate how much water ballast makes it neutrally buoyant (only the stopper just above the surface). Hint: draw the forces on it.

  2. Check your calculation: put the ball in water and draw in water with the syringe. Does the measured volume agree?

  3. Put the submarine in the unknown liquid and draw in that liquid until it is neutrally buoyant. What does this say about the liquid’s density compared with water’s?

The calculation. Measure the ball’s mass \(m\) on the scales and its diameter \(d\) with the calliper; its volume is \(V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi(d/2)^3\). Neutral buoyancy means weight equals buoyant force: the ball plus its ballast (volume \(V_b\) of the same liquid) has the mass of the liquid the whole ball displaces,

\[ \tag{37.1} m + \rho\,V_b = \rho\,V \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V_b = V - \frac{m}{\rho} \quad\text{and}\quad \rho = \frac{m}{V - V_b}. \]

Worked example

Diameter \(d = 7.00\) cm, so \(r = 3.50\) cm and \(V = \tfrac43\pi(3.50)^3 = 179.6\) cm\(^3\). Mass \(m = 60.0\) g.\ In water (\(\rho = 0.998\) g cm\(^{-3}\)): \(V_b = 179.6 - 60.0/0.998 = 179.6 - 60.1 = 119.5\) mL.\ In the unknown liquid the ball needs \(V_b = 125.0\) mL: \(\rho = 60.0/(179.6 - 125.0) = 60.0/54.6 = 1.10\) g cm\(^{-3}\), about 10 % denser than water (a strong salt or sugar solution).\ Check: the measured ballast in water is usually a few millilitres less than calculated, because the stopper stays above the surface and so displaces nothing; subtract its volume from \(V\) for a closer match.

Answer to step 3

Read it from (37.1): \(\rho = m/(V - V_b)\). The ball’s mass \(m\) and volume \(V\) are fixed, so the more ballast it needs (\(V_b\) larger), the smaller the denominator and the denser the liquid. Needing more of the unknown liquid than of water (125.0 against 119.5 mL in the example) means the liquid is denser than water; needing less would mean it is lighter.

In the atmosphere

A parcel of air is neutrally buoyant when its density matches its surroundings: it neither rises nor sinks. Weather balloons and research floats are trimmed the same way, and submarines flood or blow their ballast tanks to sink or surface.

37.3 Activity 2.3: the Cartesian diver¶

Steps

Materials: a sealed plastic bottle full of tap water containing a pipette weighted with nuts or washers, with a bubble of air trapped in it.

  1. Squeeze the bottle. Why does the half-closed pipette sink? Why does it rise when you let go? Think of the balloons.

  2. Explain the diver’s behaviour in terms of pressure and Archimedes’ principle.

Answers

1. Squeezing raises the pressure everywhere in the water (water passes pressure on but barely compresses). The air bubble in the pipette is compressed (Boyle’s law): its volume shrinks and water is pushed up into the pipette. Let go: the pressure falls, the bubble expands and pushes the water out.\ 2. The diver (glass or plastic, weights and air) displaces less water when its bubble shrinks, while its own mass gains the water that entered: its average density rises above the water’s, \(\Delta F > 0\) in (35.3), and it sinks. Released, its density falls below the water’s and it rises. A carefully held squeeze balances the two, and the diver hovers: neutral buoyancy.

How hard must you squeeze?

Suppose the diver has 2.0 mL of air and just floats, displacing 0.05 mL more than it needs. It sinks once the bubble shrinks by 0.05 mL, to 1.95 mL: by Boyle’s law the pressure must rise by a factor \(2.0/1.95 = 1.026\), that is by 2.6 %, about 2.6 kPa, a firm squeeze.

In the atmosphere and ocean

The same link between pressure, volume and density decides whether air rises or sinks: atmospheric stability (§ 35.2). Fish adjust their swim bladders, and divers their buoyancy jackets, exactly like the diver’s bubble.
