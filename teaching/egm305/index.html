EGM305 The Atmosphere

Study guides, slides and an interactive atlas for the practicals of EGM305, a second-year module at Ulster University on weather, atmospheric circulation and climate. The module is led by Prof. Derek Jackson with Dr Ambrose McCarron; these materials are my companions to the practical sheets, written while demonstrating on the module.

How to use these

Each guide follows the order of the practical sheet. Every question is first restated in plain language, then the ideas it needs are built from definitions and physical laws, then it is solved with every number shown and the answer checked for sense. Equations are cross-referenced, so whenever a formula reappears you can jump back to where it was derived.

Try each question on paper first. Cover the answer box, work it through, then compare. If your answer differs, find the step where your reasoning diverged before reading on; that step is where the learning is. The PDFs are fully hyperlinked, so they work best on a screen; the short classroom guide is designed to be printed and carried into the lab.

Practical 1: Vertical structure of the atmosphere

Pressure and density with height, the 5.6 km halving rule, partial pressure of oxygen at altitude, the standard atmosphere, lapse rates and inversions, plotting radiosonde profiles (Hillsborough and Florida), the tropopause, and why the troposphere is not the same thickness everywhere.

Practical 3: Atmospheric moisture, atmospheric motion and weather-map analysis

Humidity and the saturation vapour table, the dew point, the forces that move air, constant-pressure surfaces, geostrophic adjustment, friction and the four circulation systems, decoding station models, drawing streamlines from wind barbs, and the full analysis of a weather map: isobars, the low, fronts, cloud and rain.

Atmosphere Atlas

A single, self-contained web page: an interactive globe, which flattens to a map, that explains the weather, the oceans and the climate from first principles to graduate level. Every layer is drawn from real data (NOAA, NASA, IBTrACS, Natural Earth and others) and the page works offline once loaded.

  • A 4D globe. Rotate, zoom and move through the seasons and the hours of the day; layers for temperature, pressure, winds at four heights, the jet stream, vertical motion, water vapour, rain, cloud, sea-surface temperature, ocean currents, sea ice and climate zones.
  • 125 topics in 13 chapters, each at three levels (simple, in depth, expert with equations): the Sun and seasons, why the wind blows, the global circulation, the ocean, weather systems, tropical cyclones, ENSO and other climate modes, water, climate change, the sky, and forecasting.
  • Laboratories for the Coriolis effect, balance of forces, rising air, the Ekman spiral, Rossby waves, monsoons, depressions, storm tracks, El Niño, the energy balance, Milankovitch cycles and chaos, plus the Practical 3 laboratories.
  • Real charts with data-table twins, seven guided tours, a glossary of 217 terms, light and dark themes, unit switching and a phone layout.

The atlas is about 11 MB, because the data travel inside the page, so it can take a few seconds to open on a slow connection. A browser with WebGL is needed.

Reading

The module reading schedule follows two textbooks: Lutgens and Tarbuck, The Atmosphere: An Introduction to Meteorology, and Ahrens, Meteorology Today. For free, open alternatives:

  • Stull, R. (2017) Practical Meteorology: An Algebra-based Survey of Atmospheric Science. University of British Columbia. Free online edition.
  • The Met Office: Learn about weather pages, and its surface pressure charts for practising map analysis on today's weather.
