Student and peer feedback

At the end of an EGM505 poster-support lab I ran an anonymous Vevox poll: one 1–5 rating of how useful the session was, and open questions on what I should start, stop and continue doing.

13 responses

Mean usefulness rating 3.85 out of 5; 9 of 13 students rated the session 4 or 5.

“I like how you drop in and ask if we are doing okay every few minutes, it gives us a chance to ask questions when needed and lets us know support is nearby.”

What I changed

  • Open, proactive check-ins with every group, rather than waiting to be asked.
  • Short whole-class summaries of advice given to individual groups.
  • Key steps written on the board or in the chat so they are not lost.

From the subject advisor's observation of the EGM715 lesson: “Overall, the lecture is well-structured, engaging, and well-designed to support learning outcomes in HTML and CSS foundations.” His suggestions (shorter introductions, separate HTML and CSS sessions, code shown beside its output) are built into the next version.

How I teach

Most of my teaching happens beside a student at a bench or a screen, so I try to make that moment count: ask what they are working on, find the step where their reasoning diverged, and leave them with something written down. In materials I aim for the same thing at scale. Every question is answered from first principles, every number is shown, every symbol is defined where it first appears and cross-referenced afterwards, and practice problems come with answers so students can check themselves.

I build interactive tools where a picture moves understanding further than a paragraph: a geostrophic wind that adjusts as you change the pressure gradient, a station model that decodes as you hover, or a real weather map on which students draw their own fronts. Accessibility is part of the design from the start: readable type and contrast, light and dark themes, captions and data-table twins for charts, and pages that work on a phone.
