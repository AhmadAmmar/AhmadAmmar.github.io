30.1 One chain of ideas

The three sections of this part are one argument, and they close the argument begun in Parts I and II:

  1. Air holds water vapour up to a limit set by its temperature. Relative humidity compares the vapour with that limit; the mixing ratio and the dew point measure the vapour itself, and cooling air to its dew point makes dew, fog or cloud (Moisture Q1–Q6, Chapters 21 and 22).

  2. Warm air columns are taller than cold ones (the scale-height law of Part I, Chapter 3), so pressure surfaces slope down from the warm tropics towards the cold poles, more steeply with height (Motion Q1–Q6, Chapter 24). The uneven warming that causes this is the sun-angle effect of Part II (Chapter 18).

  3. The slope produces a pressure gradient force; on a rotating Earth the Coriolis force turns the moving air until the two balance and the wind blows along the isobars, the geostrophic wind (Motion Q7, Chapter 25).

  4. Near the ground friction slows the wind and turns it across the isobars towards low pressure, so air spirals into lows, rises, and forms cloud and rain (Motion Q8–Q11, Chapter 26).

  5. A weather map shows all of this at once: station models record the observations, streamlines show the flow, and isobars, fronts, cloud and rain show the weather systems it organises (Map Q12–Q14, Chapters 27 to 29).

30.3 Mistakes to avoid

  1. Ranking by relative humidity instead of the actual mixing ratio (Moisture Q3).

  2. Reading the dew point from the wrong row of Table 21.1, or at the wrong temperature: always check that the air could hold at least the sample’s vapour at the temperature you give (Moisture Q6: 26, not 25).

  3. Putting H and L the wrong way round on the pressure surfaces: the lower pressure surface (colder column) is the L (Motion Q4).

  4. Drawing the Coriolis force to the wrong side: right in the north, left in the south, always at right angles to the wind.

  5. Drawing the pressure gradient force away from low pressure. It points towards low pressure.

  6. Forgetting that the staff of a wind barb points to where the wind comes from (Map Q12 and Q13).

  7. Mis-reading the pressure code: 998 is 999.8, not 1099.8 or 998.0 (Map Q12).

  8. Isobars that cross, or that stop in the middle of the map (Map Q14).

30.4 Self-test

Try these first. The answers follow.

Self-test: one line each

  1. Air at 20 \(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) has a mixing ratio of 7.5 \(\mathrm{g\,kg^{-1}}\). What is its relative humidity?

  2. That air is cooled to 10 \(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\). What is its relative humidity now, and what is its dew point?

  3. Which holds more water vapour: air at 35 \(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) with RH 30 %, or air at 25 \(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) with RH 60 %?

  4. Find the dew point of air at 30 \(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) and RH 50 %, and the height at which cloud would form if it were lifted.

  5. True or false: the dew point can be higher than the air temperature. Explain.

  6. Why do pressure surfaces slope down towards cold air?

  7. The 300 mb surface is at 8700 m over place S and 9500 m over place M. In which direction does the pressure gradient force act at 9000 m?

  8. Two 500 hPa height lines 90 m apart are 600 km apart at 35\(^{\circ}\)N. Find the geostrophic wind.

  9. In the Southern Hemisphere a wind blows towards the west. Where is the low pressure, to its north or south?

  10. What does friction do to (a) the speed and (b) the direction of the surface wind?

  11. Over land the surface wind is 8 \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and crosses the isobars at 40\(^{\circ}\). Estimate the geostrophic wind.

  12. Air spirals inward and clockwise. Which hemisphere, which pressure system?

  13. Decode the pressure codes 087 and 962.

  14. Convert 50 \(^{\circ}\mathrm{F}\) and 14 \(^{\circ}\mathrm{F}\) to \(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\).

  15. A wind barb has two full barbs and one pennant. What is the speed?

  16. Behind a front the temperature falls 18 \(^{\circ}\mathrm{F}\) and the wind turns clockwise from south-west to north-west. Which kind of front?

  17. A staff points to the north-east. In which direction does the air move?

  18. The 1012 mb isobar lies between stations at 1009.6 and 1013.2 hPa. Where?

  19. Why does a strong wind go with closely spaced isobars?

  20. What can relative humidity not tell you?

Answers to the self-test

  1. \(7.5/14.956=50\,\%\).

  2. \(w_s(10)=7.762\): RH \(=7.5/7.762=97\,\%\). Dew point: Table 21.1 gives 6.771 at 8 \(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) and 7.762 at 10 \(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\), so \(T_d=8+2(7.5-6.771)/(7.762-6.771)=9.5\,\ensuremath{^{\circ}\mathrm{C}}\).

  3. \(w_s(35)\approx37.3\) gives \(0.30\times37.3=11.2\,\ensuremath{\mathrm{g\,kg^{-1}}}\); \(w_s(25)\approx20.5\) gives \(0.60\times20.5=12.3\,\ensuremath{\mathrm{g\,kg^{-1}}}\). The 25 \(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) air holds more.

  4. \(w=0.5\times27.694=13.85\,\ensuremath{\mathrm{g\,kg^{-1}}}\): between 18 \(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) (13.162) and 20 \(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) (14.956), \(T_d=18+2(13.85-13.162)/1.794=18.8\,\ensuremath{^{\circ}\mathrm{C}}\). Cloud base \(\approx125\times(30-18.8)=1400\) m.

  5. False (in open air): it would mean the air is oversaturated and the surplus vapour would condense at once. At most \(T_d=T\) at saturation.

  6. Cold air is denser, so a column of it is thinner: the same pressure drop uses up less height, so the pressure surfaces are lower over cold air.

  7. From the high (the 300 mb surface is higher over M, so z over M has the higher pressure) to the low: from M towards S.

  8. \(f=2\times7.292\times10^{-5}\sin35\ensuremath{^{\circ}}=8.37\times10^{-5}\); \(V_g=\dfrac{9.81\times90}{8.37\times10^{-5}\times6\times10^{5}}=17.6\,\ensuremath{\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}}\) (34 kt).

  9. To the north: in the Southern Hemisphere the low is on the right of the wind (Buys-Ballot), and the right of a westward wind is north.

  10. (a) It reduces the speed. (b) It turns the wind across the isobars towards low pressure.

  11. \(V=V_g\cos\alpha\), so \(V_g=8/\cos40\ensuremath{^{\circ}}=10.4\,\ensuremath{\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}}\).

  12. Inward and clockwise: Southern Hemisphere low.

  13. \(087\to1008.7\) hPa; \(962\to996.2\) hPa.

  14. \(50\,\ensuremath{^{\circ}\mathrm{F}}=10\,\ensuremath{^{\circ}\mathrm{C}}\); \(14\,\ensuremath{^{\circ}\mathrm{F}}=-10\,\ensuremath{^{\circ}\mathrm{C}}\).

  15. \(2\times10+50=70\) knots.

  16. A cold front.

  17. The air moves towards the south-west.

  18. \((1012-1009.6)/(1013.2-1009.6)=0.67\): two thirds of the way from the lower to the higher station.

  19. Close isobars mean a large pressure gradient force, which needs a large wind to balance it with the Coriolis force (and friction).

  20. How much vapour is actually in the air (or how the air compares with air at another temperature): it only compares the vapour with the capacity at the present temperature.

30.5 A checklist

  • I can look up, and interpolate, in Table 21.1.

  • I can find an RH, a mixing ratio and a dew point from any two of \(T\), \(w\), RH.

  • I can explain why pressure surfaces slope and why wind increases with height.

  • I can draw the three forces correctly in both hemispheres.

  • I can name the four systems of Figure 26.5 from their rotation.

  • I can decode a station model: pressure, temperature, dew point, sky, wind, weather, tendency.

  • I can draw streamlines (arrows downwind) and isobars (interpolating, never crossing).

  • I can say how to recognise a front, and which way the wind turns across it.
