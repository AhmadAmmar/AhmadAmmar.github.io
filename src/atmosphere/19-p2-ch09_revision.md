## 19.1 One chain of ideas {#p2-sec:chain}

The five questions are one argument, each step resting on the one before:

1.  Everything above absolute zero radiates, over a spread of wavelengths described by the Planck curve (Chapter [12](/teaching/atmosphere/p2-ch02_radiation/#p2-ch:radiation)).

2.  The **peak** of that spread moves to shorter wavelengths as temperature rises (Wien’s law): the Sun, at about 5800 K, peaks in visible light; the Earth, near 288 K, in the thermal infrared (Question 1, Chapter [13](/teaching/atmosphere/p2-ch03_wien/#p2-ch:wien)).

3.  The **total** emission rises as the fourth power of temperature (Stefan–Boltzmann law): twenty times the temperature means 166 000 times the emission (Question 2, Chapter [14](/teaching/atmosphere/p2-ch04_stefan/#p2-ch:stefan)).

4.  A planet settles at the temperature at which it emits as much as it absorbs. That temperature depends on its distance from the Sun (inverse-square law) and its albedo, and can be read from its spectrum (Question 3, Chapters [15](/teaching/atmosphere/p2-ch05_spectra/#p2-ch:spectra) and [16](/teaching/atmosphere/p2-ch06_planets/#p2-ch:planets)).

5.  Where the actual temperature is higher than this, an atmosphere is trapping infrared (the greenhouse effect: Venus, the Earth) or the planet has its own heat (Jupiter). For the Earth the greenhouse effect is worth about 33 K (Question 4, Chapter [17](/teaching/atmosphere/p2-ch07_balance/#p2-ch:balance)).

6.  Across the Earth’s surface, the energy received depends on the angle of the Sun. The tilt of the axis moves that angle through the year and so makes the seasons (Question 5, Chapter [18](/teaching/atmosphere/p2-ch08_sunangle/#p2-ch:sun)).

This heating explains the structure met in Part I: a troposphere heated from below and a stratosphere heated by ozone (Part I, § [5.4](/teaching/atmosphere/p1-ch04_temperature/#p1-sec:layers)), thickest over the tropics (Part I, § [7.4](/teaching/atmosphere/p1-ch06_lapse/#p1-sec:q11)). Part III shows how the uneven heating from equator to pole fills the air with moisture and sets it in motion.

## 19.3 Mistakes to avoid {#p2-sec:mistakes}

- **Using Celsius in a radiation law.** $\sigma T^{4}$ and $C/T$ need kelvin. Convert first, and convert back only at the end (§ [14.5](/teaching/atmosphere/p2-ch04_stefan/#p2-sec:q2c)).

- **Forgetting the fourth root.** When solving $E=\sigma T^{4}$ for $T$, divide by $\sigma$ *and then* take the fourth root, Eq. ([17.3](/teaching/atmosphere/p2-ch07_balance/#p2-eq:Tinv)); a single square root gives a value about 65 000 instead of 255.

- **Mixing up the sun angle and the zenith angle.** The sun angle is from the horizon, the zenith angle from the vertical; they add to $90^\circ$, Eq. ([18.1](/teaching/atmosphere/p2-ch08_sunangle/#p2-eq:beta)). Beam spreading uses the sun angle.

- **Calculator in radians.** $\sin(83.5)$ in radian mode gives 0.97 instead of 0.9936.

- **Subtracting percentages and calling the result a percentage.** Two intensities of 80.4 % and 11.3 % differ by 69.1 percentage points; in relative terms one is 7.1 times the other (§ [18.6](/teaching/atmosphere/p2-ch08_sunangle/#p2-sec:q5c)).

- **Reading the peak of a coarsely sampled spectrum as exact.** The highest data point lies near, not at, the peak (§ [15.4](/teaching/atmosphere/p2-ch05_spectra/#p2-sec:q3b)).

## 19.4 Self-test {#p2-sec:selftest}

<div class="box box-try"><p class="box-title">Self-test: one line each</p>


1.  Why is the Sun’s radiation called shortwave and the Earth’s longwave?

2.  If a star is three times as hot as the Sun, how does its total emission per square metre compare?

3.  Why does Venus have a lower black-body temperature than the Earth but a far higher surface temperature?

4.  Why do we divide the solar constant by four?

5.  Without an atmosphere, would the Earth be colder by day, by night, or both?

6.  Why is there little seasonal change in temperature at the equator?

</div>

<div class="box box-answer"><p class="box-title">Answers to the self-test</p>


1.  Wien’s law: the Sun is about 20 times hotter, so its emission peaks at a wavelength about 20 times shorter (0.5 against 10 $\mu\mathrm{m}$) (Chapter [13](/teaching/atmosphere/p2-ch03_wien/#p2-ch:wien)).

2.  $3^{4}=81$ times as much, Eq. ([14.2](/teaching/atmosphere/p2-ch04_stefan/#p2-eq:ratio4)).

3.  Its bright clouds reflect most sunlight (high albedo), so it absorbs little; but its thick CO$_2$ atmosphere traps the infrared from the surface (§ [16.7](/teaching/atmosphere/p2-ch06_planets/#p2-sec:q3e)).

4.  The Earth catches sunlight on a disc of area $\pi R^{2}$ but spreads it over a sphere of area $4\pi R^{2}$ (Figure [16.1](/teaching/atmosphere/p2-ch06_planets/#p2-fig:disc)).

5.  Mostly by night: nights would be far colder. Days could be hotter, as on the Moon (§ [17.8](/teaching/atmosphere/p2-ch07_balance/#p2-sec:q4f)). On average it would be about 33 K colder.

6.  The noon Sun is always high there (at least $66.5^\circ$), so the intensity changes little through the year (Practice Set [18](/teaching/atmosphere/p2-ch08_sunangle/#p2-ch:sun), item [18](/teaching/atmosphere/p2-ch08_sunangle/#p2-ch:sun).2).

</div>
