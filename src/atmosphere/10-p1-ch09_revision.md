## 10.1 One chain of ideas {#p1-sec:chain}

The fourteen questions are one argument, each step resting on the one before:

1.  Air has weight, and pressure is the weight of the air above. Because air is compressible, the air near the ground is squeezed by everything above it: pressure and density fall **exponentially** with height, halving roughly every 5.6 km (Questions 1–2, Chapter [3](/teaching/atmosphere/p1-ch02_pressure/#p1-ch:pressure)).

2.  Reading that curve gives the pressure anywhere; because the proportion of oxygen is the same at all heights, its **partial pressure** falls in step, to a third on Everest (Questions 3–4, Chapter [4](/teaching/atmosphere/p1-ch03_oxygen/#p1-ch:oxygen)).

3.  Temperature falls through the troposphere, on average by 6.5 $^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ per km, then stops falling at the **tropopause**, above which the ozone-heated stratosphere begins (Question 5, Chapter [5](/teaching/atmosphere/p1-ch04_temperature/#p1-ch:temperature)).

4.  Real soundings follow the standard atmosphere only roughly: they contain **inversions**, and their tropopauses lie at different heights (Questions 6–8, Chapter [6](/teaching/atmosphere/p1-ch05_soundings/#p1-ch:soundings)).

5.  The troposphere is **thickest and its top coldest over the tropics**, where heating and convection are strongest and warm air columns are tallest (Questions 9–11, Chapter [7](/teaching/atmosphere/p1-ch06_lapse/#p1-ch:lapse)).

6.  Equal height steps remove equal *fractions* of the air, so pressure falls fast low down and slowly high up; and air that sinks warms by compression, so the cold air aloft is held up: the atmosphere is **stably layered** (Questions 12–13, Chapter [8](/teaching/atmosphere/p1-ch07_stability/#p1-ch:stability)).

7.  Near the ground, the surface and the winds over it control the temperature: an ocean warmed by the Gulf Stream keeps Northern Ireland mild (Question 14, Chapter [9](/teaching/atmosphere/p1-ch08_climates/#p1-ch:climates)).

Part II explains where the heat comes from in the first place, and Part III how the differences of pressure set up here make the air move.

## 10.4 Mistakes to avoid {#p1-sec:mistakes}

- **Halving the height instead of the air.** Each 5.6 km step halves the *percentage*; the heights go up in equal steps.

- **Joining the halving-rule points with straight lines.** The rule describes a smooth exponential curve; straight segments make the readings of Question 3 wrong between the points.

- **Mixing metres and kilometres** in the lapse rate or in $z/5.6$.

- **Getting the sign of the lapse rate wrong.** Temperature falling with height is a *positive* lapse rate.

- **Taking any warm step as the tropopause.** A thin inversion low in the troposphere (Hillsborough, 1.4 km) is not the tropopause: the temperature falls again above it.

- **Saying the air aloft is “heavier” because it is colder.** At the same pressure, yes; at its own low pressure it is far lighter.

## 10.5 Self-test {#p1-sec:selftest}

<div class="box box-try"><p class="box-title">Self-test: one line each</p>


1.  Why does pressure fall faster near the ground than high up?

2.  Why does the air on Everest have less oxygen, though it is still 21 % oxygen?

3.  What is the difference between an inversion and the tropopause?

4.  Why is the tropical tropopause the coldest?

5.  Why does the cold air of the upper troposphere not sink?

6.  Why is Northern Ireland milder in winter than Newfoundland?

</div>

<div class="box box-answer"><p class="box-title">Answers to the self-test</p>


1.  Each height step removes the same fraction of the air above, and near the ground there is far more air above (§ [8.1](/teaching/atmosphere/p1-ch07_stability/#p1-sec:q12)).

2.  Each breath holds a third as many molecules; the oxygen partial pressure is a third of that at sea level (§ [4.5](/teaching/atmosphere/p1-ch03_oxygen/#p1-sec:q4)).

3.  An inversion is any layer where the temperature rises with height; the tropopause is the top of the troposphere, above which the temperature stops falling for good (§ [6.5](/teaching/atmosphere/p1-ch05_soundings/#p1-sec:tropopause)).

4.  It is the highest, so the temperature falls over the most kilometres before it stops (§ [7.4](/teaching/atmosphere/p1-ch06_lapse/#p1-sec:q11)).

5.  It is far less dense than the air below, and if it sank it would warm by compression and become lighter still (§ [8.4](/teaching/atmosphere/p1-ch07_stability/#p1-sec:q13)).

6.  Westerly winds off an ocean warmed by the Gulf Stream, against a cold current and a frozen continent upwind of Newfoundland (§ [9.4](/teaching/atmosphere/p1-ch08_climates/#p1-sec:q14)).

</div>
